Why a positive test is usually a false alarm

It sounds backwards, but it happens whenever a condition is rare and the test makes even a few mistakes. You'll see why with dots. No maths needed yet.

A test for a condition is 90% accurate. Only 1 in 100 people have the condition. You take the test and it comes back positive.
What's the chance you actually have it?
"90% accurate" here means: it catches 90 of every 100 sick people, and wrongly flags 10 of every 100 healthy people.

Now the formula, with your numbers

This is Bayes' theorem. It's exactly what the dots just did. Hover a coloured piece to light up its dots.

In a crowd of 10,000: 4 sick & positive104 positives in total ≈ 3.48%
In probabilities: P(sick | positive) = 90% × 0.04%90% × 0.04% + 1% × 99.96% = 0.036%1.04% = 3.48%

Top: sick people who test positive. Bottom: sick & positive + healthy & positive (false alarms). In words: the share of positives that are real. Statisticians write it P(H|E) = P(E|H)·P(H) / [P(E|H)·P(H) + P(E|not H)·P(not H)].

The idea in one breath

When few people have the condition, even a small false-alarm rate adds up to more healthy people flagged than sick people caught. So a positive result is more likely to come from the large healthy group. Make the condition common, or the test never cry wolf, and the answer jumps.
