# Would a heat pump save me money versus my gas boiler?

**Short answer for your house (sections 5–6): yes, but only if you move to a heat pump tariff. On your current standard variable tariff the answer is no.** The general method and example are in sections 1–4.

All prices are in generic "currency units per kWh" so the maths works anywhere. The example values are illustrative assumptions, not quotes.

## 1. The break-even rule

A boiler turns 1 kWh of gas into about 0.85–0.92 kWh of heat. A heat pump turns 1 kWh of electricity into SCOP kWh of heat. SCOP is the seasonal coefficient of performance, typically 2.8–4.0 for a well-designed UK or European install.

Running costs are equal when:

```
electricity price / gas price  =  SCOP × boiler efficiency
```

- If your price ratio is **below** SCOP × boiler efficiency, the heat pump is cheaper to run.
- If it is **above**, the gas boiler is cheaper to run.

Example: SCOP 3.2 × boiler 0.85 = **2.7**. If electricity costs less than 2.7 times as much as gas per kWh, the heat pump wins on running costs.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 per kWh (standard tariff) | 911 | 850 | 729 | 637 |

| 0.15 per kWh (heat pump tariff) | 546 | 510 | 437 | 383 |

Compare these with 840 for gas:

- **At 0.25 per kWh** (price ratio about 3.6), the heat pump only breaks even at a SCOP of about 3.0 and saves roughly 100–200 at SCOP 3.5–4.0.
- **At 0.20 per kWh** (ratio about 2.9), it saves roughly 100–330 per year.
- **At 0.15 per kWh** (ratio about 2.1), it saves roughly 300–450 per year, even with a mediocre SCOP.

Things that shift the result further:

- **Gas standing charge.** If you disconnect gas entirely, you also save that fixed daily charge, often 100–150 per year.
- **Solar PV, a battery or time-of-use tariffs.** These lower your effective electricity price.
- **Government grants.** These are not running costs, but they change the payback (see section 3).

## 3. Upfront cost and payback

Running-cost savings only matter relative to what you pay to switch.

| Item | Typical range (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Air-source heat pump, installed | 8,000–15,000 |
| Radiator upgrades, bigger cylinder, pipework | 1,000–5,000 |
| Grant or subsidy (where available) | minus 5,000–7,500 |
| Replacement gas boiler you'd otherwise buy | minus 2,000–3,500 |

```
simple payback (years) = (net heat pump cost − avoided boiler cost) / annual running-cost saving
```

Example: net cost after grant 6,000, avoided boiler 2,500 and savings of 300 per year gives (6,000 − 2,500) / 300, or about **12 years**. With no grant and savings of 150 per year, it is far longer. Without a saving, there is no payback on cost alone.

## 4. Factors that move the answer

- **Insulation and heat loss.** A leaky house needs more heat, which means higher flow temperatures and a lower SCOP. Insulating first often pays off more than the heat pump does.
- **Emitters.** Existing radiators sized for 70 °C flow may need replacing to run at 40–50 °C. Underfloor heating suits heat pumps best.
- **Hot water.** Heating a cylinder to 50–60 °C lowers efficiency. The effect is small if the cylinder is well-managed.
- **Climate.** Colder design temperatures lower the seasonal SCOP.
- **Maintenance.** Heat pumps need an annual service, much like a boiler. Costs are similar.
- **Non-financial factors.** Carbon emissions fall substantially, especially as the grid decarbonises. Comfort is steady but slower to respond. Outdoor space and noise rules apply.
- **Price risk.** Electricity-to-gas price ratios differ by country and change over time. A policy shift that moves levies from electricity to gas would improve the case.

## 6. Verdict

**Revised after stress-testing (section 7): not now on money alone. Yes when the boiler needs replacing, or now only if you can lock in a heat pump tariff of about 14p or less.** This tightens my first answer. The simple payback figures below look fine, but they ignore discounting, servicing costs and the fact that the heat pump wears out after about 15 years.

- **Standard variable tariff (26p):** **No.** Your electricity-to-gas price ratio is 4.3 (26p / 6p), and a heat pump at SCOP 3.0–3.6 needs it to be below 2.5–3.0 to win. You'd pay about the same or slightly more.
- **Heat pump tariff at about 14p:** **Roughly break-even over 15 years** (net present value about −£600 to +£200 on the numbers in section 7). It becomes clearly positive (+£1,500 to +£2,300) if you count the boiler replacement you avoid.
- **Heat pump tariff at 17p:** **No**, unless you count the avoided boiler replacement, and then only just.

### This is a good-value move, not an urgent one

Your boiler works, so the strongest case comes later when it needs replacing. If the grant is still available then, that is the best moment. The £7,500 scheme is current, but I can't promise it will last, so factor in that risk.

### Before you commit

1. **Get 2–3 MCS-certified quotes** that include a room-by-room heat loss survey and a stated SCOP. Walk away from anyone who won't give one. The grant is claimed through the installer, and the voucher lasts three months.
2. **Aim for a net cost under about £7,000 and a design SCOP of 3.2 or better.** If quotes come back well above that, the payback stretches past 15 years and I'd wait.
3. **Check the live tariff rates** with the supplier before relying on 14p. My search results for current heat pump tariffs ranged from 7p to 17p and came from low-quality comparison sites, so treat 14p as a placeholder.
4. **Consider topping up your loft insulation to 270 mm.** It costs a few hundred pounds and mainly helps comfort and the heat pump's sizing. As a money saver it's small, about £20–£35 a year on gas (see section 7).
5. **Decide about gas.** Disconnecting saves about £110 a year, but only works if you have no other gas appliances.

### The break-even prices

- **Running cost break-even:** the heat pump costs less to run than gas+26p electricity only if the tariff is below about **22.5p (SCOP 3.0), 23.6p (3.3) or 24.6p (3.6)**. That's what the 26p row shows: no saving.

- **With credit for the avoided boiler replacement:** if you count a £2,500 replacement in year 5 (the boiler would then be 17), the tariff needs to be about **16.1p, 16.9p or 17.7p**.

### What moves the answer most

- **Installation price.** At 14p and SCOP 3.3, each £1,500 on the net cost moves the NPV by £1,500. At £5,000 net it's +£1,345, at £8,000 it's −£1,655 (no boiler credit).
- **Tariff.** Each 1p moves the annual saving by about £65–£75, because the tariff covers all your electricity.
- **Energy price drift.** If gas rises 1% a year faster than electricity, the 14p/3.3 case moves from −£155 to +£334. At 2% it's +£868. Conversely, if levies move off electricity onto gas, the case improves, and if gas gets cheaper, it worsens.
- **Fair baseline.** I credited you with the full 26p-to-14p drop on household electricity. You could instead stay on gas and fix at about 24p, which cuts the saving by about £58 a year. At 14p and SCOP 3.3 that gives £493.
- **Time-of-use tariffs (e.g. Octopus Cosy).** With about 80% of the heat pump's use in the cheap windows, the saving is similar to a flat 14p (around £220–£300 a year on my assumptions) and needs effort to achieve. Only worth it if you have a buffer tank or a flexible schedule.
- **Loft top-up from 100 mm to 270 mm.** Assuming it cuts heat demand by 3–5%, it saves about £20–£35 a year on gas or £12–£20 on a heat pump. It won't pay back on its own, but it's cheap and helps comfort.
- **Carbon.** Gas heating emits about 2.1 tonnes of CO₂ a year (11,500 kWh × 0.183 kg). At SCOP 3.3 and a grid of about 0.1 kg/kWh, a heat pump emits roughly 0.3 tonnes, saving about 1.7–1.8 tonnes a year. If that matters to you, it's worth more than the financial margin here.

### What would change my mind

- A quote that nets out below about £5,500 after the grant.
- A heat pump tariff you can lock in at 12p or less.
- Your boiler failing or becoming uneconomic to repair.
- A survey showing flow temperatures of 45 °C or below, which supports SCOP 3.6.
- Evidence that your gas use includes a lot of non-heating demand (cooking, gas fire) that would stay on gas, which would shrink the heat load to replace.
