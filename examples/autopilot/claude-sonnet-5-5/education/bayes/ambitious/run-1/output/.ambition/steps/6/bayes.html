Why a positive test is usually a false alarm

It sounds backwards, but it happens whenever a condition is rare and the test makes even a few mistakes. You'll see why with dots. No maths needed yet. (Made-up numbers, for learning. Not medical advice.)

A test for a condition is 90% accurate. Only 1 in 100 people have the condition. You take the test and it comes back positive.
What's the chance you actually have it?
"90% accurate" here means: it catches 90 of every 100 sick people, and wrongly flags 10 of every 100 healthy people.

The idea in one breath

When few people have the condition, even a small false-alarm rate adds up to more healthy people flagged than sick people caught. So a positive result is more likely to come from the large healthy group. Make the condition common, or the test never cries wolf, and the answer jumps.

Before you take this to a real test result

The numbers here are made up for learning. This isn't medical advice. If you've had a real positive result, don't wave it away. A positive screening result means check again, not probably nothing, and a second positive changes the odds a lot. Your own chances also depend on your age, family history and symptoms, and tests can miss sick people too. Ask your doctor what your result means for you.
